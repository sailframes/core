How the sea-breeze tactics page works

A full, plain-language explanation of every number on the Race briefing and the logic behind the calendar, stats, and replay. Everything here runs on public NOAA/EUMETSAT data with no proprietary forecast — it's a transparent, reproducible pipeline.

1. The pipeline in one paragraph

Every hour of the sailing season we read the latest HRRR weather-model run over a box from Gloucester to Provincetown, plus live observations from mid-bay buoy 44013 and inland airport KBED. A classifier labels each historical day by what its sea breeze did (frontal flip, reinforcement, etc.). The analog finder then asks: “which past days looked meteorologically most like today?” and reports what those days did — giving you a probability the breeze fills, when, and from where. All data lives as static files on a CDN and is queried directly in your browser (DuckDB-WASM) — there is no server doing the math.

3. The day classifier — what type of sea-breeze day was it?

For every warm-season day the classifier computes a handful of physical quantities, then assigns one label. “Onshore” at mid-bay 44013 means wind from the 060–170°T sector (roughly E through S — off the water).

Inputs it measures each day

  • Morning gradient — vector-mean wind 06–10 LT at 44013 (the background flow before any breeze).
  • ΔT (land−sea) — KBED daytime max temp (10–17 LT) minus 44013 sea-surface temp (10–14 LT). The engine that drives a sea breeze.
  • Insolation — HRRR mean solar radiation (DSWRF) and cloud (TCDC) over the water, 09–14 LT.
  • Afternoon trajectory & onset — wind direction at 12/14/16/18 LT, and the first time (10–18 LT) the wind holds onshore ≥ 5 kt for two consecutive readings = the onset.
  • Tide phase — hours between breeze onset and the nearest Boston high water.

The five day types

TypeDefinition (first-cut thresholds)
F — Frontal flipMorning wind offshore (190°–050°), then rotates into the onshore sector, sustained ≥ 2 h at ≥ 5 kt between 10–18 LT, with a direction change ≥ 90°. The classic Marblehead sea breeze that “fills against” the morning gradient.
R — ReinforcementMorning S–SW ≤ 12 kt that simply builds and settles onshore (≈ 150°–200°) in the afternoon — no big rotation; the gradient and the breeze point the same way.
P — Pinned inshoreCoastal stations (KBOS/KBVY) flip onshore ≥ 2 h but mid-bay 44013 never does — the breeze only fills close to shore. Tactically valuable local knowledge.
G — Gradient-dominatedDaily mean ≥ 15 kt with no onshore rotation — the synoptic wind overpowers any sea breeze.
N — NoneNone of the above fired; no organized fill.
U — Insufficient dataMissing the buoy data needed to classify (e.g. buoy adrift).

Bow-position and direction use GPS course when moving and the sector test above otherwise. Buoy/airport observations are the truth; the HRRR fields are context, not ground truth.

4. The analog finder — “what does today most resemble?”

The Race briefing takes today’s conditions and finds the historical days that were meteorologically closest, then reports what those days actually did. This is the same idea a veteran tactician uses (“this feels like that day two summers ago”) made quantitative.

The feature vector

Each day is turned into a small vector of numbers:

[ grad_u, grad_v, ΔT, SST, sin(DOY), cos(DOY) ]

  • grad_u, grad_v — the morning gradient wind split into east/north components (so direction and speed both count).
  • ΔT — land–sea temperature difference (for today, from the HRRR forecast; for history, from observations).
  • SST — sea-surface temperature.
  • sin/cos of day-of-year — encodes the season smoothly (so late-June and early-July are “close”).

How “closest” is computed

  1. Candidate pool. Only historical days within ±45 days-of-year of today, with all features present, and a real type (not U).
  2. Standardize. Each feature is z-scored (subtract the pool’s mean, divide by its standard deviation) so that a 5 °C ΔT swing and a 5 kt wind swing carry comparable weight — no single feature dominates by its units.
  3. Distance. Straight-line (Euclidean) distance in that standardized space. This is the dist shown on each matched-day card — smaller means more similar; 0 would be identical.
  4. Pick k = 30. The 30 nearest days form the analog set.

What the outcome numbers are

  • Fill onshore % — fraction of the 30 analogs that developed a sea breeze (F + R + P).
  • F / R / P / N–G % — the type breakdown of the analogs.
  • Onset p25–p50–p75 — the spread of onset times (local) among the analogs that filled.
  • Fill direction — the vector-mean onset wind direction of those fill-days.
  • Matched days — the actual dates, closest first. Highlighted ones (▶) have an archived wind field you can replay.

Cloud/DSWRF are displayed as inputs but not yet used in the distance, because the historical labels don’t all carry a cloud value — see caveats.

5. Today's forecast feed

Hourly in season, we pull the latest HRRR run: the analysis (F00, current state) plus the next 18 hourly forecast steps (F01–F18) over the venue box, and publish them as today/latest.parquet. The “Today’s forecast” button on the briefing plays that field on the map, so you can watch the model’s predicted sea-breeze development for the rest of the day. Today’s feature vector (gradient from obs-so-far, forecast ΔT, cloud, SST) is published as today/briefing.json and feeds the analog finder above.

7. Caveats & honest limitations

This is a decision aid, not a guarantee. A 53% “fill onshore” means about half of the most-similar past days filled — read it as odds, not certainty.
  • Thresholds are first-cut. The day-type boundaries (onshore sector, rotation angle, speed cut-offs) haven’t yet been tuned against a hand-labelled validation set, so the F/R/N split in particular may shift as they’re calibrated. Treat the type as indicative.
  • Cloud isn’t in the match yet. Historical labels were built without the HRRR cloud field, so cloud/DSWRF are shown but excluded from the distance until those fields are backfilled.
  • Replay covers only recent days. The animated wind-field archive is built forward one day at a time, so most historical analog days have a label but no field to replay (their cards are dimmed).
  • “Pinned inshore” (P) rarely fires yet — it needs the coastal KBOS/KBVY feed, which isn’t wired in.
  • Data gaps happen — e.g. buoy 44013 was offline for part of April 2026 (those days show as U).
